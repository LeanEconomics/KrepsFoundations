# Chapter 6 source map

David M. Kreps, *Microeconomic Foundations I*, Chapter 6, printed pp. 123–147.
The entries below cover the numbered definitions and propositions, all fourteen
problems, and the chapter’s mathematical examples and commentary. The
[corrections and conventions](corrections.md) explain the relevant qualifications.
The [chapter library map](../README.md) links each named module to its Lean source.

| Prizes, lotteries and preferences, p. 123 | `MonetaryLotteries`, `MonetaryPreference`, `LotteryMixtureRepresentation` | The three primitive mixture axioms yield a prize utility on the actual prize type. Every lottery has finite support, including on infinite intervals. Weak and strict comparisons have Chapter 5’s orientation. |
| Proposition 6.1, p. 123 | `MonetaryLotteries`, `MonetaryPreference` | Strict preference for larger sure prizes iff strict increase. Includes the primitive comparison between actual point-mass lotteries. |
| Proposition 6.2, pp. 123–124 | `PrizeContinuity` | Both directions of the stability criterion against every fixed lottery, in neighborhood and metric radius forms. Binary lotteries attain intermediate utility levels without assuming continuity. |
| Continuity in probabilities and prizes, pp. 124–125, footnotes 2–3 | `LotteryLimits`, `VaryingSupport`, `EscapingPrizes` | Fixed finite support and coordinatewise probability convergence, bounded extra prizes on varying supports, moving probabilities and prizes, the literal nearby two-prize examples, weak convergence against bounded continuous utility and common compact confinement. The rare extreme prize gives an actual weak limit and a strict preference reversal. |
| Proposition 6.3, p. 125 | `MonetaryLotteries`, `MonetaryPreference`, `RiskAttitudes` | Concavity iff preferring every sure mean to its simple lottery. Risk seeking corresponds to convexity and risk neutrality to affine mixture equalities. Concavity gives continuity in the interval’s interior. |
| Definition 6.4, Proposition 6.5, Figure 6.1, pp. 125–127 | `MonetaryLotteries`, `MonetaryPreference`, `RiskAttitudes` | A certainty equivalent is a sure prize indifferent to the lottery. Continuity ensures existence, strict increase gives uniqueness, and both together characterize risk aversion by nonnegative premia and risk neutrality by zero premia. Figure 6.1 uses the actual 0.6/0.4 weights. |
| Definition 6.6 and Proposition 6.7, pp. 127–129 | `ComparativeRisk`, `WealthShifts` | The full lottery-versus-sure-prize comparison iff concavity of the inverse utility transform on the attained range. Includes the equivalent ordering of all certainty equivalents and premia. |
| Proposition 6.8 and inverse formulas, pp. 129–130 | `ArrowPrattComparison`, `ArrowPrattTheorem`, `InverseUtilityDerivatives`, `IntervalArrowPratt` | Both directions of the pointwise absolute-risk comparison. The inverse and its transform inherit smoothness at interior wealth and satisfy the printed first- and second-derivative formulas. `proposition_6_8_within` needs only derivatives within the common interval, including its endpoints. |
| Proposition 6.9 and Definition 6.10, p. 130 | `LocalRiskPremium`, `RiskMeasures` | The local inverse constructs an actual certainty equivalent of the equal-chance symmetric gamble. Its risk premium divided by the squared stake tends to half the Arrow–Pratt coefficient. The proof uses the Taylor remainder at the base wealth and permits locally affine utility. |
| Definition 6.11 and Proposition 6.12, pp. 130–131 | `WealthShifts`, `WealthRiskClassification`, `ConstantAbsoluteRisk`, `IntervalWealthShifts`, `IntervalCARA` | The original admissible prize overlap is explicit for every pair of wealth shifts. Falling, rising and constant premia are characterized by the corresponding coefficient behavior. Solving the differential equation gives the positive affine transform of negative exponential utility, and the positive affine function of wealth when the coefficient is zero. Relative derivatives suffice on closed endpoints. |
| Proportional gambles and the relative coefficient, pp. 131–132 | `RelativeRisk` | For positive gross returns, the certainty-equivalent return falls with wealth iff relative risk aversion is nondecreasing. Negative exponential utility refutes the printed opposite direction. |
| Definition 6.13 and order properties, p. 132 | `StochasticDominance`, `DominanceOrders` | Universal tests over all nondecreasing utilities for first order and over all nondecreasing concave utilities for second order. Both are partial orders on the actual finite-support laws. |
| Proposition 6.14, p. 132 | `QuantileBins`, `QuantileCoupling`, `DominanceInterpolation`, `IntegratedDominance` | First-order dominance iff the CDF is weakly lower everywhere. Second-order dominance iff every printed interval integral is weakly lower, starting at a point where both CDFs vanish. Includes the equal lower and upper limit. |
| Proposition 6.15, p. 133 | `FiniteCouplings`, `QuantileCoupling`, `CouplingSeparation`, `SupermartingaleCoupling`, `ConditionalRisk` | The ordered coupling and the coupling with nonpositive conditional drift both have the actual marginal laws. Normalized conditional means and mass-weighted inequalities agree on the positive support. The supermartingale coupling is derived from dominance for arbitrary finite supports and real probabilities. |
| Figure 6.2 and footnote 10, pp. 133–135 | `DominanceExample` | One common six-branch law produces the printed initial, final and supplement distributions. Conditional mean supplements are 0, −3/4 and 0. The three-prize initial law second-order dominates the four-prize final law. |
| Proposition 6.16, pp. 134–137 and footnote 12 | `IncomeChoice`, `EqualProbabilityRisk` | Given the resulting continuous von Neumann–Morgenstern utility, every optimal selection after income is observed induces the same expected indirect utility and the three mixture axioms. Chapter 5 supplies the primitive representation theorem. At fixed positive prices indirect utility is continuous in nonnegative income, strictly increasing under local insatiability and concave under cardinal concavity. Continuity and midpoint comparisons suffice for the stated concavity test. |
| Joint prices and income, pp. 137–138 | `PriceIncomeRisk` | The two literal joint laws have the same marginals at nominal incomes 10,000 and 20,000 and prices p and 2p. One produces purchasing power 10,000 in both states, the other 5,000 or 20,000. Independent draws give the price-averaged utility and its income properties. At fixed income the index is continuous and nonincreasing in prices. |
| Proposition 6.17 and (6.1)–(6.2), pp. 138–140, footnotes 13–14 | `Insurance`, `InsuranceOptimality` | The exact expected utility is concave in unrestricted real coverage. The first-order marginal utility condition characterizes global optimality. Fair full insurance is optimal and loaded premia imply strictly less than full coverage at every optimum. At fair prices all coverages tie for affine utility. A sufficiently expensive contract has no maximizer. |

| 6.8(a–b), p. 143 | `PriceRiskExamples` | For linear tastes, actual optimal purchases imply strict preference for the printed risky prices. At zero income all alternatives tie. |
| 6.9, p. 143 | `ImperfectInsurance` | The answer depends on utility. One genuinely imperfect, actuarially fair loss law is shared by two strictly increasing strictly concave differentiable utilities. Full insurance is optimal for one and fails the necessary marginal condition for the other. |
| 6.10 and Proposition 6.18(a–f), pp. 144–145 | `RiskyAssets`, `PortfolioCases` | Exact expected utility for nonnegative investment without an upper bound. | 6.11(a–c), p. 145 | `CARAPortfolio` | A below-safe return in the actual support prevents an optimum at infinity. A positive mean excess return gives a positive finite, unique position. The optimum equals a fixed positive position divided by absolute risk aversion, independently of wealth. |
| 6.12, p. 145 | `MultipleRiskyAssets` | Explicit portfolios with no short sales show why a negative-premium hedge is demanded and why a positive-premium dominated asset can be excluded. The full feasible menus and actual maximizing choices enter the examples. |
| 6.13(a–b), pp. 145–146 | `SmallShares` | Positive derivative at zero yields strictly preferred small shares under the stated pricing rule. Independent random wealth retains the conclusion. A kinked utility and an adverse correlation with initial wealth give counterexamples to the proposed extensions. |
| 6.14 and optional challenge, pp. 146–147 | `RepeatedGambles`, `StoppedGambles`, `StoppedGambleLimits`, `StoppedRuinProbability`, `StoppedBoundedUtility` | For the literal +1,000/−500 gamble with probabilities 0.4/0.6, a strictly increasing consumer rejects every positive fixed horizon. With bankruptcy stopping at zero and initial wealth 2,000, every strictly increasing utility unbounded above eventually accepts. Bounded increasing utilities exhibit both decisions. With wealth k in 500-dollar units, eventual ruin probability is qᵏ for q=(√7−1)/2. The limiting expected utility is L+(U(0)−L)qᵏ for bounded increasing utility with supremum L, giving the eventual decision whenever the limit differs from current utility. |

markets, financial practice and later strategic models provide motivation. The
probability laws, choice implications, comparative statics, numerical examples and
counterexamples linked above are the mathematical coverage. The independent-package
prerequisites and original revisions are listed in the [third-party notices](../THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES.md).
